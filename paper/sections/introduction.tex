\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Retailers record sales, not demand. When a product sells out, the customers who could not buy it leave no trace in the data~\cite{fildes2022retail}. A model fitted to such records learns the stocking policy along with customer behavior, and in fresh retail its errors go straight into the next order, which has to balance lost sales against perishable surplus~\cite{nahmias1994demand,huh2011adaptive}. A lower forecast error is therefore not enough to show that one ordering policy is better than another. A fair comparison needs the same outcome, the same cost ratio, and the same scored rows.

FreshRetailNet-50K~\cite{wang2025freshretailnet,dingdong2025datasetcard} makes such a comparison possible because it stores an hourly stock-status flag next to hourly sales. We call a series-day with no flagged stockout between 06:00 and 21:59 a \emph{donor day}; if the flags are correct, its recorded sales over that window equal demand. Donor days are not a random sample, and they tell us nothing about days that were actually censored. They do, however, allow a controlled experiment, much like the recovery evaluation in the dataset report~\cite{wang2025freshretailnet}. We hide part of the sales on donor days with three simulated stockout mechanisms, rebuild features and forecasts from each censored history, and score every forecast and order against the uncensored proxy. A model trained under one mechanism can then be tested under another on exactly the same rows.

The results come from three saved runs of one notebook pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:lineage}). Two development runs prepare the data, simulate censoring, and tune the models: run~S on a stratified sample of 5,000 series and run~P on all 50,000 series. Run~E repeats these steps on its own 5,000-series sample and then adds the neural and Kaplan--Meier baselines, calibration, ordering, a protocol lock, and a single evaluation of the final week. Only run~E is used in the final evaluation.

The paper contributes an evaluation design and the evidence it produces rather than a new forecasting model. Earlier work on this dataset reports demand recovery and forecast accuracy~\cite{wang2025freshretailnet} or profit measured on censored sales~\cite{azar2026pipeline}. We make three contributions. First, we score ordering cost against an uncensored proxy, so that nine policies are compared on identical rows. Second, we vary how the training history was censored and measure what it costs to move a model between mechanisms, with paired cluster-bootstrap intervals. Third, we show that the ranking of calibrated demand quantiles and a raw-sales model reverses with the cost ratio in every train/test cell, and we bound the miss rate of the 90\% quantile forecast (q90) on real censored days.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Classical methods for censored demand fit a parametric distribution to the observed sales~\cite{nahmias1994demand} or learn order levels nonparametrically with the Kaplan--Meier estimator~\cite{kaplan1958nonparametric,huh2011adaptive}; our Kaplan--Meier baseline follows the second route. Besbes and Muharremoglu~\cite{besbes2013implications} show what censoring hides about the optimal order, Sachs and Minner~\cite{sachs2014datadriven} adapt the data-driven newsvendor to censored samples, and Kumar and Mouchtaki~\cite{kumar2026value} show that treating sales as demand can degrade badly as censored data accumulate. Feature-based and neural newsvendor methods learn orders directly from covariates~\cite{ban2019bignewsvendor,bertsimas2020predictive,oroojlooyjadid2020deep}. With linear costs the optimal order is a quantile, which links ordering to quantile regression~\cite{koenker1978regression}.

LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} was used by practically all of the top 50 entries in the M5 accuracy competition~\cite{makridakis2022m5}, and simple models often match transformer forecasters~\cite{zeng2023transformers}. We also use TimesNet~\cite{wu2023timesnet} to recover masked hours and the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)~\cite{lim2021tft} for next-day forecasts. Our calibration builds on conformalized quantile regression~\cite{romano2019cqr}, adaptive conformal inference~\cite{gibbs2021adaptive}, and monotone rearrangement~\cite{chernozhukov2010quantile}, and Cao~\cite{cao2024conformal} applies conformal calibration to the feature-based newsvendor. Conformal guarantees assume exchangeability or bound the departures from it~\cite{barber2023conformal}, and real censored days satisfy neither. On FreshRetailNet-50K itself, the dataset report~\cite{wang2025freshretailnet} evaluates recovery on synthetically masked non-stockout periods with an official baseline~\cite{dingdong2025baseline} that we could reproduce only in part, and Azar~\cite{azar2026pipeline} selects newsvendor policies but scores profit on censored sales, whose bias relative to demand cannot be identified. Other stockout-aware studies exclude out-of-stock days from training and evaluation~\cite{lebedev2026stockout} or feed a global forecaster into a cost-aware ordering rule~\cite{szablowski2026vn2}. None of them scores orders against an uncensored outcome while varying the censoring mechanism.
