\section{Locked Final-Week Evaluation}
\label{sec:final}

The final evaluation scores donor rows only. Each of the nine transfer cells (one per pair of training mechanism and evaluation history) contains the same 20,641 final donor series-dates, and the six full-sample policies other than Kaplan--Meier are scored on all of them. Kaplan--Meier is scored only where its order is estimable, on 6,012 to 20,641 rows per cell, and the two neural policies on the 2,510 final donor rows of the deep subset. We impute no predictions for the remaining 4,400 series.

\subsection{Policy comparison}
\label{sec:policies}

Table~\ref{tab:policies} lists the mean costs of the A-trained models on the A history. Which full-sample policy is cheapest depends on the cost ratio. The raw-sales LightGBM with residual quantiles wins when excess stock is more costly and, narrowly, at equal costs (0.3868 against 0.3891 for pooled conformal), whereas the pooled-conformal policy wins when shortage is more costly. The B history gives the same ranking. On the C history, pooled conformal is already the cheaper of the two at equal costs (0.4094 against 0.4411). Kaplan--Meier is the most expensive full-sample policy in every setting. In the deep subset, TFT on the recovered history costs less than direct TFT in every matched history and setting.

Table~\ref{tab:contrasts} reports the paired contrasts fixed in the lock, pooled over the three matched histories. Calibration lowers cost compared with the uncalibrated demand quantiles in all three settings, and pooled conformal is cheaper than the seasonal-naive and Kaplan--Meier policies throughout. Against the raw-sales LightGBM with residual quantiles, the sign depends on the cost ratio: pooled conformal costs more when excess stock is more costly, the equal-cost interval contains zero, and it costs less when shortage is more costly. Raising $\beta$ from 0.25 to 0.75 increases the pooled-conformal fill rate on the A history from 0.614 to 0.891 and the leftover share from 0.056 to 0.226, and the C history is the most expensive in every setting. Calibration moves the q90 coverage of the 20,641 matched rows from 0.798--0.828 to 0.879--0.887 and lowers the q90 pinball loss from 0.119--0.131 to 0.109--0.114. Coverage still falls short of the nominal 0.90, and it says nothing about censored days.

\subsection{Mechanism transfer and robustness}
\label{sec:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} fixes the pooled-conformal policy at $\beta=0.75$ and varies the history used for training and calibration. The transfer gap $\Delta_{m\to h}=\overline{C}_{m\to h}(\mathcal I)-\overline{C}_{m\to m}(\mathcal I)$ compares the mean cost on an unseen evaluation history $h$ with the matched cost on the same rows $\mathcal I$. The largest gap occurs for models trained on A and evaluated on C: 0.7303 against 0.6831, a gap of $+0.0472$. The C-trained models cost less on the A and B histories than on their own, which is the hardest, so a negative gap points to an easier history rather than to better transfer. All intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from 200 cluster-bootstrap replicates~\cite{efron1979bootstrap} over the 866 city--store clusters, with the same draws on both sides of a contrast. Dates are not resampled separately and there is no multiplicity adjustment, so we treat the intervals as descriptive.

The cost-ratio reversal holds in all nine train/test cells. When excess stock is more costly, the raw-sales LightGBM with residual quantiles is the cheapest full-sample policy in every cell, with pooled conformal 0.0007 to 0.0323 more expensive. When shortage is more costly, pooled conformal is the cheapest in every cell, 0.0441 to 0.1298 below the raw-sales policy. At equal costs, four cells go to each of the two and one to the uncalibrated demand quantiles. Across the three settings, the largest transfer gap of pooled conformal ($+0.0472$) is the smallest worst case among the six policies whose forecasts read the evaluation history; the raw-sales quantile LightGBM reaches $+0.2258$, and Kaplan--Meier orders do not read that history at all. Over the seven final dates (2,507 to 3,385 donor rows each), the C history is the most expensive on every date, and the daily q90 coverage of pooled conformal ranges from 0.839 to 0.907. Under-coverage is concentrated in series with at most ten complete fit-period days (0.752 to 0.769 over 532 rows) and on promotion days (0.854 to 0.865). These breakdowns are descriptive and were not pre-specified.

\subsection{Real recorded histories}
\label{sec:real}

We applied the q90 forecasts of the A-, B-, and C-trained pooled-conformal models to the same 35,000 real final-week rows, including rows with flagged stockouts. The definite-miss lower bounds~\eqref{eq:miss} are 0.0895 $[0.0852, 0.0942]$, 0.0854 $[0.0808, 0.0896]$, and 0.0724 $[0.0684, 0.0766]$. A smaller bound can simply come from a higher forecast, so on its own it does not mean better calibration. The bound is highest for rows with 1--4 recorded stockout hours (0.1465 to 0.1797) and lowest for rows with 9--16 hours (0.0298 to 0.0334): when a product sells out early, recorded sales rarely exceed the forecast even when demand does. Only the complete-day stratum has an exact proxy.
